Dispatch I · Letters & liberty

[[abigail.category]]
Abigail Adams · Braintree, Massachusetts

[[abigail.title]]
Remember

[[abigail.title_accent]]
the ladies.

[[abigail.date]]
Filed the 8th of June, 1776

[[abigail.place]]
Braintree

[[abigail.body_one]]
Mrs. Abigail Adams has put a most inconvenient question to the gentlemen who speak so readily of liberty. In a letter of the 31st of March to her husband, now serving in Congress, she urges that the making of new laws should not leave women subject to unchecked authority at home.

[[abigail.body_two]]
“Remember the Ladies,” she writes. I find those few words worthy of their own broadside. If a people would resist arbitrary power in a king, ought they not examine its exercise within the household also?

[[abigail.more_label]]
Read the correspondent’s reflection

[[abigail.more]]
Mrs. Adams asks for particular care in the laws that may follow an American separation from Britain. I would not pretend that her letter promises a place for women in Congress. Yet a question once committed to paper is not easily recalled: whose liberty shall this struggle secure?

[[abigail.source_label]]
Consult the letter · 31 March–5 April 1776

[[abigail.source_url]]
https://www.masshist.org/digitaladams/archive/doc?id=L17760331aa

[[mercy.eyebrow]]
Dispatch II · The political stage

[[mercy.category]]
Mercy Otis Warren · Plymouth, Massachusetts

[[mercy.title]]
A sharper

[[mercy.title_accent]]
sort of weapon.

[[mercy.date]]
Filed the 10th of June, 1776

[[mercy.place]]
Plymouth

[[mercy.body_one]]
There are weapons in this quarrel which require neither powder nor shot. Mrs. Mercy Otis Warren employs ridicule. In The Group, printed in 1775, she turns the ambitions of the Crown’s adherents into a farce, inviting readers to judge the men who would govern them.

[[mercy.body_two]]
Her political writings have appeared without her name. Such concealment need not signify a timid mind. A woman may be denied a seat in the assembly and still place an argument before every gentleman who opens a newspaper.

[[mercy.more_label]]
Read the correspondent’s reflection

[[mercy.more]]
The Warrens’ home in Plymouth is connected with the Patriot cause, and Mrs. Warren corresponds with political friends, including the Adams family. I shall not mistake the drawing room for a legislature; nevertheless, opinions formed there may travel farther than their authors are permitted to go.

[[mercy.source_label]]
Read The Group · published 1775

[[mercy.source_url]]
https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/29224

[[phillis.nav_label]]
Phillis Wheatley

[[phillis.eyebrow]]
Dispatch III · Poetry & freedom

[[phillis.category]]
Phillis Wheatley · Boston, Massachusetts

[[phillis.title]]
Let her verse

[[phillis.title_accent]]
be heard.

[[phillis.date]]
Filed the 12th of June, 1776

[[phillis.place]]
Boston

[[phillis.body_one]]
Miss Phillis Wheatley’s Poems on Various Subjects, Religious and Moral appeared in London in 1773. Carried from Africa into enslavement as a child, she has made a place in letters that many persons would have denied her before reading a single line.

[[phillis.body_two]]
Last October she addressed a poem to General Washington. His reply, dated the 28th of February, acknowledges her talent and thanks her for the verses. A cause so fond of the word liberty must surely consider the claims of those whom slavery has wronged.

[[phillis.more_label]]
Read the correspondent’s reflection

[[phillis.more]]
I offer no invented account of a meeting with the General. The surviving correspondence is sufficient testimony to this exchange. Nor shall praise for a poet excuse the bondage endured by others: the liberty of these colonies cannot be measured by fine phrases alone.

[[phillis.source_label]]
Read Washington’s reply · 28 February 1776

[[fiction.nav_label]]
Ruth Whitcomb · Fiction

[[fiction.eyebrow]]
An imagined dispatch · Ordinary labours

[[fiction.category]]
Ruth Whitcomb · A fictional print-shop worker

[[fiction.title]]
The hands

[[fiction.title_accent]]
behind the news.

[[fiction.date]]
An imagined day in June, 1776

[[fiction.place]]
Boston

[[fiction.body_one]]
Before the first bundle of papers reaches the street, Ruth Whitcomb has counted sheets, sorted battered type, and cleared yesterday’s ink from the work-table. Her employer’s name will stand upon the imprint. Her own will scarcely be noticed beyond the shop door.

[[fiction.body_two]]
This morning she carries the finished sheets through a shower, protecting them beneath her cloak whilst the hem of her gown grows heavy with rain. The town shall have its arguments by breakfast. Ruth must wait until the deliveries are done.

[[fiction.note]]
Fictional person and episode. Ruth Whitcomb is invented to explore ordinary labour; this is not a recovered biography, quotation, or eyewitness account.

[[about.nav_label]]
The correspondent

[[about.eyebrow]]
From the writing desk

[[about.title]]
Your humble

[[about.title_accent]]
correspondent.

[[about.category]]
Eleanor Hartwell · An imagined reporter

[[about.body_one]]
Good reader, I am Eleanor Hartwell, a Boston gentlewoman with more questions than patience for their neglect. I keep this small gazette to observe the part women take in our present troubles: by correspondence, by composition, and by the daily labour upon which public affairs depend.

[[about.body_two]]
I lay no claim to an impartial temper where arbitrary power is concerned. Yet an honest pen must distinguish a thing known from a thing imagined. Let the reader consult the papers gathered at the close of this issue.

[[about.note]]
Editorial note: Eleanor is fictional. All dispatches and filing dates are modern reconstructions written in a period-inspired voice. The three historical women and cited documents are real; no interviews are claimed.
